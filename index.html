Python · Quantitative research

Trading Lab

A Python research engine built to test whether algorithmic cryptocurrency strategies remain credible under demanding validation.

01 / Research system

A workbench, not a scoreboard

The project is designed for developing and evaluating strategies across multiple timeframes. Its interface keeps normalized data, experiment metadata, and synchronized event evidence close together.

The emphasis is not simply on finding profitable historical results. Fees, data leakage, look-ahead bias, and execution assumptions remain visible so that weak ideas can be rejected early.

02 / Experiment archive

Every result has a place

Exploratory research and execution-aware backtests remain separate catalog entries. Each record connects a strategy to its dataset, research phase, signal count, clustering output, and completion state.

This structure makes comparison easier without allowing a later result to quietly replace an earlier artifact.

03 / Provenance

Evidence behind the experiment

Dataset lineage, validation status, stored counts, calculation versions, and run identifiers make each result inspectable. The detail view records what entered the pipeline and how far it progressed.

Walk-forward testing, Monte Carlo analysis, and structured metrics matter most when the inputs behind them can still be traced.

04 / Execution reality

The gap after costs

Gross and net results are shown together so the effect of fees and execution assumptions cannot disappear behind a headline return. The distance between the two is part of the strategy, not a footnote.

05 / Decision rules

How an idea leaves the market

Exit-rule comparisons expose how strategy behavior changes when the decision boundary moves. Testing those alternatives against the same interval helps separate a durable signal from a convenient parameter choice.

06 / Event context

One timeline, several views

Price, external fair value, mark, oracle, and stored signals are aligned around the same event window. Missing observations remain gaps instead of being silently connected or filled.

07 / Liquidations

Pressure made visible

Liquidation evidence adds context that price alone cannot provide. Bursts of forced activity can be inspected beside the same stored interval used by the strategy.

08 / Market state

The conditions around a signal

Liquidity state describes the environment in which a signal appears. That surrounding market structure helps distinguish similar price movements that occurred under very different conditions.

09 / Trade flow

From observation to judgment

Executed market activity completes the event view. Together, the synchronized panels turn a strategy result into something that can be inspected, challenged, and either advanced or discarded.

Roblox Studio · Luau

Naval Simulator

A real-time naval sandbox built around large ocean environments, detailed ships, and the systems that make a moving vessel feel inhabited.

01 / System foundations

The Ship and the Sea

The first task I undertook in this project was modeling my ship, a Ticonderoga-class guided-missile cruiser. After I had the model, I brought it into Roblox Studio and set up kinematics using a mathematical wave-surface model to determine the ship’s vertical position at any point. On top of this, the ship uses spring physics to mimic the natural motion of a vessel atop a wave.

The second task was designing the ocean. I went through a few iterations before ultimately landing on triangle tiles. I achieved this by sampling my mathematical wave model at various points and using those points as vertices. Points closer to the player’s ship are sampled more densely and updated faster. This allows me to avoid rendering the ocean on the server and optimize it for each client’s machine.

02 / A floating base

The Player Is Not the Ship

My second challenge was allowing the player to walk around the ship. In the Roblox engine, every player inherits a character model, and the camera is attached to this model. My game makes the player model invisible and sticks it to the ship, making it appear as though the camera is orbiting the ship itself. To integrate first-person movement within the ship, I had to create a new camera system, detaching the camera from the player model and positioning it inside the vessel. The WASD movement keys are rebound in first-person view to move this camera.

Once I had the camera set up, I had to deal with collisions, stairs, and doors. Rather than calculating collisions from the imported 3D ship model, I created simple collision volumes covering all surfaces on the ship. These serve as the actual collision model. This is much less computationally expensive and gives me more freedom when fixing collision bugs.

03 / Weapons systems

The Fun Part

The third thing I integrated was the weapon systems. The Ticonderoga class carries a number of weapons; the most important to this project are the Mark 41 Vertical Launching System, Mark 45 five-inch guns, and Phalanx CIWS. While the Ticonderoga has additional armaments, these were the first to be integrated into my game. To get the weapons functional, I first had to develop a radar-targeting system. My system uses simple raycasts to determine what it can see and renders their endpoints to the player as a rudimentary terrain-detection system. If a raycast can see an enemy, that enemy is shown on the radar and can be targeted. By checking the coordinates of a targeted ship, I can calculate the angle needed to orient the gun mount and the ballistic trajectory needed to set its elevation. Firing the gun moves a bullet part along that trajectory, causing damage if it makes contact with a ship.

A bullet fired by the system is just a part with special properties; a missile is a little more complicated. A missile is its own entity, like a ship, with an identity and a radar signature. This allows players to monitor their own missiles and detect incoming missiles using radar, which becomes very important when the CIWS needs to track and shoot them down. The missile-firing system queries the radar in the same way as the gun system. Once it obtains the target’s coordinates, it calculates and follows a trajectory. Having the missile obtain target information from the radar also allows me to integrate electronic-warfare systems such as jammers later on.

With these two systems in place, setting up the CIWS was just a matter of leveraging my existing gun-orientation code and doing some simple rigging on the CIWS model.

04 / Details

Interior Spaces Give the Ship Life

Once I had the basic systems in place, it was time to work on detailing the interiors. First, I gave the ship a cargo hold and a boiler room. Repairs in the boiler room cost supplies, represented by stacked boxes in the cargo hold. This hold is also intended to house a helicopter in later versions. Working in this confined space was made much easier by using my collision volumes; calculating collisions from complex 3D models is buggy and computationally expensive, especially when building interiors. This was also a stress test for my door system and a chance to change the rendering engine so water does not render inside the cargo hold.

After tackling the cargo hold and boiler room, I remodeled the bridge interior. Designing the bridge became more than just a modeling job. The bridge should be the central gameplay location for the ship’s crew, so it needed to be visually appealing and functional. I ended up with a three-console bridge: space for a captain, an electronic-warfare specialist, and a weapons specialist. After modeling the bridge, I made the screens interactive, allowing the captain to drive the ship and use the radar entirely in first person.

05 / Sensor development

Making Contact Data Readable and Jammable

Unsatisfied with my first-person radar interaction, I dove into a 3D radar representation. This view renders a 3D model of radar space, complete with a field-of-view circle that conforms to blocking terrain. On this foundation, I started building basic electronic-warfare systems. The jammer floods a targeted area with radar noise, showing false positives on radars within the area and making effective targeting impossible. This affects both ships and missiles.

This system is prettier, easier to interact with, and less computationally expensive than my previous 2D radar representations. Jammers and radar systems can also be damaged or knocked offline by successive hits.

Blender · Animation · Visual development

Animation Projects

A growing collection of animation, motion, and visual-development studies made in Blender.

01 / Building the scene

Form before finish

The work begins with structure: broad forms, spatial relationships, and camera-readable silhouettes. This ship scene is still visibly inside the working viewport, where modeling and composition can evolve together.

These recordings are process material rather than polished reels. They preserve the decisions, revisions, and imperfect intermediate states that lead toward a finished shot.

02 / Motion study

Testing the beat

A short render is useful for checking timing without committing to a long sequence. Even a few frames can expose a pause that feels too long, a move that lands too softly, or a composition that stops reading once it is in motion.

This compact study sits alongside the larger development recordings as a quick checkpoint between viewport work and a more complete animation.

03 / Light and atmosphere

Finding the image

This vehicle study started with a reference picture of a truck. The final result is a fully rigged physics driven model with realistic suspension and interior details.

I made this truck to be used as a hero prop in a short-film 3D animation, the clip on the right is a pre-rendered preview of a scene.

04 / Staging

Character in context

The final recording brings character, environment, and camera together. Staging is treated as part of the animation itself: posture, framing, contrast, and surrounding geometry all influence how the moment is read.

Detailed production notes are still being prepared. For now, this continuous set of studies shows the work where it actually happens—between an initial blockout and the finished image.

05 / Environment

Weather in motion

Rain changes more than the background. Falling water, surface response, haze, and shifting contrast work together to give the environment a stronger sense of depth and movement.

This study brings those elements into one animated scene, using weather to turn a static landscape into a living environment.
